Most people think fitness starts with the hard stuff. The heavy sets, the sessions that leave you flat on the floor. Walking barely registers. It’s the warm-up, or the thing you do on a rest day.

Walking is the base your training sits on. Everything else stacks on top of it.

What your body is built to do

You’re built to move at low effort for long stretches. You’ve got Achilles tendons that work like springs, storing energy and giving it back with each step. The glutes keep you upright over distance. And you sweat, which cools you while you keep moving and is something almost no other animal does well, so you can cover ground in heat that would stop most animals cold.

For most of human history that meant a lot of walking. Tracking, gathering, carrying loads, getting from one place to the next on foot. People who study how humans evolved put daily movement somewhere around 10 to 15 kilometres a day, none of which anyone thought of as a workout.

Then came chairs, cars, desks and lifts. Now the average person walks a fraction of that and calls three 30-minute sessions a week active. Those sessions matter, but they’re sitting on top of a base that’s gone missing.

Why the base matters more than the session

You can train hard three times a week and still spend the other 23 hours a day barely moving. The training is real work and it does real things for you. It still can’t undo a day spent mostly in a chair.

Walking fills that gap. It keeps your joints moving through their range without loading them up, moves blood around, and helps you recover between sessions. It burns a bit of fuel without leaving you starving at dinner. It clears your head. And you can do it every day without needing to recover from it, which you can’t say for a hard session.

It’s also an honest test of whether your fitness works in the real world. Plenty of people can grind through a class and then get winded on a flight of stairs. If you’re moving well across a whole day, the training is landing where it should.

How to do it

You don’t need a programme. You need to move more, most days.

Start from where you are. If you’re on 3,000 steps a day, don’t chase 20,000 tomorrow. Aim for 6,000 this week. Walk to the things you’d usually drive to. Take your calls on your feet. Get off the tram a stop early. Walk after dinner instead of dropping straight onto the couch. Let the dog or the kids or a podcast be the reason you get out the door.

The number on your phone is a rough guide. Some days you’ll clear 12km without thinking about it. Some days you’ll get 4,000 steps and a hard session, and that’s fine too. What matters is the pattern over weeks rather than the score on any one day.

None of this is the exciting part of training. There’s no photo in it. It’s the part everything else is built on, and it’s the easiest thing on the whole list to start today. Put your shoes on and go for a walk.
