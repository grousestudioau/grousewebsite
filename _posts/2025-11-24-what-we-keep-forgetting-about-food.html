There’s a line from Hippocrates everyone likes to quote:
“Let food be thy medicine.”
Most people stop reading there, because it feels wise enough to screenshot and stick on Instagram.

What he meant was pretty ordinary. Eat in a way that supports living, not just getting through the day.

The old approach to food was boring and uncomplicated. You ate when you were hungry, you ate what was around, and you avoided the stuff that left you feeling like death the next morning.

Somehow we’ve turned eating, the most basic thing there is, into either a science experiment or a guilt competition. Macro calculators, red-listed foods, a fear of bread, ten-step morning routines with chia seeds that cost more than rent.

If food rules make your eyes glaze over, you’re built like every generation before the last five minutes of human history.

The ancients didn’t need weight-loss challenges or “don’t eat after 7pm.” They paid attention to themselves. They noticed what worked and what didn’t, and they treated appetite, fullness and movement as part of the same system. Not in a woo-woo way. In a practical one.

They ate to feel capable. To wake up with energy, to get through the day’s work and still think straight. Food was fuel for doing things.

Somewhere along the way we forgot that eating isn’t a personality trait. It’s a basic input for a body that has to do things. We handed our intuition over to apps and influencers who have no idea what your life looks like.

If you want a real rule from the old days, try this one: eat in a way that lets you do the things you say you want to do.

Lifting heavy? You need to eat enough to back it up. If feeling lighter on your feet is the goal, eat so you’ve got energy left over instead of a food coma. And consistency comes from making food convenient before you make it perfect.

What Hippocrates was getting at was a way of eating that fits the life you live, rather than a fantasy version of it.

Keep it simple. Eat regularly, eat enough, and pay attention to how it lands. That’s the old approach, and it still works better than any six-week meal plan.
